Let's go deep on PL2-2Center — because this transformer deserves a thorough look, and the engineering picture here has several layers worth understanding. Start with the field condition. PL2-2Center is a 2,500 kVA transformer at Foster Farms, Livingston HQ. It's serving a large-scale food processing and cold storage operation — which means it never fully rests. The BT view confirms this: even during quieter operational periods, the load never collapses completely, because the refrigeration systems maintaining cold storage temperatures are always drawing power. This is a 24-hour-a-day, 7-day-a-week load environment, and everything you see in this data compounds continuously. The power factor picture is the central problem. At 83.9 percent average — with a maximum of 90.9 percent and a minimum that touches zero during the interruption event — this transformer is carrying a reactive burden that averages 150.5 kVAR and peaks at 311 kVAR. To put 311 kVAR in physical terms: at that peak moment, the reactive current moving through those transformer windings is approaching the same magnitude as the productive real current. That reactive current does no work — it just circulates, heats the windings, and occupies the kVA capacity of your transformer that could otherwise be available for load growth. The thermal consequence is directly measured. At 158,890 BTU per hour, this transformer is radiating waste heat at a rate that your facility cooling infrastructure has to continuously manage. That cooling cost runs approximately $4.78 per hour — roughly $41,867 per year — just to deal with the thermal output of an inefficient field. In a California facility managing summer ambient temperatures, that cooling load doesn't operate in isolation. It adds to your HVAC burden during the months when cooling costs are already at their peak. Now let's talk about the voltage field, because it tells a stress story that goes beyond the reactive numbers. Average voltage of 482.7 volts sounds stable, but the recorded minimum of essentially zero tells you something important happened during this period. We identified one field interruption event — a complete voltage dropout lasting an estimated 5 to 15 minutes, most likely near the mid-point of the observation window. When the field came back, the reconnection inrush peaked at 520.7 amps. For context: the average operating amperage on this transformer is 331.7 amps. That 520-amp inrush represents a 57 percent surge above normal operating current, absorbed by a transformer with no reactive compensation cushioning the event. Every motor, every variable-frequency drive, every contactor on this circuit experienced that transient. And the voltage field shows continuous stress even absent that outage event. The persistent triangular drop patterns — those recurring V-shaped voltage dips — are the fingerprint of motor inrush and compressor cycling throughout the production day. They don't concentrate in one shift or one week; they're distributed continuously. That tells you the load events generating them are operational constants — they're built into how this facility runs. In an untuned field, each of those dips lands harder on the voltage rail, because there's no reactive compensation available to support it during the inrush. The cumulative effect on motor insulation, drive IGBT stress, and contactor contact wear is real and measurable over time. The harmonic picture adds one more layer. Average iTHD of 5.3 percent is manageable. The 10.4 percent peak is worth watching.
