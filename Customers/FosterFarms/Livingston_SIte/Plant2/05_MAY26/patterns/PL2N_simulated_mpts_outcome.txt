**PL2N — Simulated MPTS Correction Outcome**

The S view projects what the PL2-3North field looks like with MPTS correction active — and the transformation is substantial. With one H490 unit installed and delivering up to 400 kVAR of reactive compensation, the power factor climbs from the current 60.7 percent average to a projected 99.5 percent. That single number reframes the entire operating picture.

At 99.5 percent power factor, the reactive current burden that currently accounts for a large fraction of conductor loading is absorbed locally at the point of compensation rather than being drawn through the full upstream infrastructure. The transformer, conductors, switchgear, and upstream metering all see a dramatically reduced apparent current for the same real work output. Thermal burden drops in proportion to the reduction in reactive current squared — the physics of resistive heating make reactive elimination particularly high-leverage for thermal relief.

The planning-level estimate for one H490 unit installed at this transformer is approximately $200,000, subject to final engineering scope and site conditions. That installed investment recovers approximately $131,870 per year in direct energy waste, with the broader all-in waste figure of approximately $213,478 per year representing the full opportunity. Simple payback on the direct waste recovery alone lands in the range of 18 to 24 months at planning level — a strong return for capital equipment with a 20-plus year service life in a well-maintained industrial environment.

Beyond the financial recovery, MPTS correction on PL2-3North addresses the root cause of the voltage sag clustering observed in the V view. By reducing the reactive current draw during load switching events, the field becomes more voltage-stable — the triangular drop signatures that indicate motor-start sag events become shallower and less frequent. That translates directly to reduced stress on VFD input stages, longer motor winding insulation life, and lower probability of nuisance tripping on sensitive process controls during shift starts and equipment cycling sequences.
