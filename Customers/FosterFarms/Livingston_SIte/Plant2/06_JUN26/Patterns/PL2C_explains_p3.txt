Let's go deep on PL2C — because this transformer deserves the full picture, and the full picture has several layers that build on each other. Start with where the field stands today. Your 2500 kVA transformer at Livingston HQ ran through June with an average power factor of 86.5 percent. The maximum power factor at any point during the month was 93.9 percent — meaning even at its best, this field never achieved a balanced reactive state. At its worst, power factor dipped to zero, which in a one-minute resolution dataset usually indicates a brief period of zero-load condition or a measurement boundary. The sustained operating average of 86.5 percent is what matters for waste accounting. Reactive demand averaged 141.9 kVAR across the month. The MAX reactive excursion reached 276.8 kVAR. To put that in context — at 276.8 kVAR peak, you are pushing approximately 276,800 volt-amperes of reactive energy back and forth through your transformer windings and distribution cabling every second during those peak events. That energy does no work. It moves product on no conveyor belt, refrigerates no product, heats no processing vessel. It exists purely as electromagnetic burden — heating iron, heating copper, and forcing your cooling infrastructure to work continuously against it. The thermal consequence is direct and measurable. At 139,059 BTU per hour of thermal burden, with cooling consuming 20.4 kilowatts at roughly $4.03 per hour — in a June environment at Livingston where ambient temperatures are already climbing — your cooling system is working against a man-made heat source that MPTS correction would largely eliminate. The voltage health story compounds everything else. What the V view captured across thirty days is a field that oscillates constantly, dips sharply and repeatedly, and recorded a minimum excursion of 9.7 volts. Let me be direct about that number: 9.7 volts on a 480-volt nominal bus is a momentary collapse. It almost certainly represents a switching transient or a hard motor fault event that lasted only seconds — but it was real, it was captured, and it occurred in an environment where connected equipment includes refrigeration compressors, processing motors, variable frequency drives, and sensitive electronic process controls. The recurring triangular dip pattern visible throughout the month tells you these aren't isolated events. They are repeating, clustered, and correlated with load cycling. Each dip-and-recovery cycle stresses motor insulation through thermal cycling, stresses drive electronics through voltage transient injection, and stresses contactor and relay coil pull-in voltage margins. Across thirty days of continuous operation, that is a meaningful cumulative wear load. The harmonic layer adds a third heat source on top of the reactive and switching losses. An iTHD of 5.3 percent average peaking at 10.6 percent means harmonic currents — primarily third, fifth, and seventh harmonics generated by variable frequency drives and rectifier loads — are circulating through your transformer windings continuously. Harmonics don't show up on a kilowatt-hour meter in the way real power does, but they absolutely show up in winding temperature. A 10.6 percent iTHD peak is at the upper boundary of what NEMA transformer derating tables treat as acceptable without thermal uprating. Combined with the reactive burden already present, the transformer is running hotter than its nameplate rating accounts for. Now let's turn to correction. The MPTS simulation view projects this field at 99.5 percent power factor post-correction. Reactive demand drops from 276.8 kVAR MAX to 52.8 kVAR MAX — a 224 kVAR reduction at peak. Average reactive demand falls from 141.9 kVAR to 22.3 kVAR. Those aren't marginal improvements. That's a fundamental restructuring of how current moves through this transformer — from a reactive-dominated, thermally burdened field to a near-unity, work-efficient field. Sizing for this correction is one H490 unit. The rule is kVAR MAX divided by 400, rounded up — 276.8 divided by 400 is 0.69, which rounds to one H490 at 400 kVAR capacity. That gives you correction headroom above the current June peak, which is important heading into July and August when refrigeration compressor demand at a food processing facility typically increases. If seasonal load growth pushes peak reactive demand above 400 kVAR, a second unit would be needed — this is worth a seasonal projection review before finalizing the engineering scope. Projected annual savings from correction are approximately $101,062 per year, against a planning-level installed investment of roughly $200,000 — subject to final engineering scope and site conditions, not a binding figure. That implies a payback window of approximately two years. In a field that is currently degrading equipment, generating waste heat, and running about $106,209 per year in measurable losses, a two-year payback for a correction system that then runs for fifteen to twenty years is a straightforward value proposition.
