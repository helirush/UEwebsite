The June average was 79.8 percent. The theoretical maximum is 100 percent — meaning every volt-amp delivered to the transformer becomes productive kilowatts of real work. At 79.8 percent, you are achieving that on less than four out of every five volt-amps delivered. The remaining fraction — and it is a meaningful fraction — is reactive current. It circulates. It heats things. It expands the apparent power demand. And your utility charges you for it. The reactive load in numbers: 698.6 kVAR average, peaking at 1,132.9 kVAR maximum. To frame that peak — your real power peak demand was 1,295.2 kilowatts. So at the reactive peak, you were carrying reactive current nearly equal in magnitude to your entire real production load. The transformer was, in effect, serving two full-size loads at once — one doing work, one just circulating heat. The current burden that creates is substantial. The M view confirms a peak amperage of 1,693.3 amps. Every amp above what the real load actually requires is traveling through every conductor, every connection, and every winding in the circuit — adding heat at every step. That heat accumulates into a thermal burden of approximately 885,600 BTU per hour. I want you to picture what that means concretely. It is equivalent to roughly 74 tons of air conditioning demand — generated purely by electrical inefficiency on this one transformer. And your facility's cooling systems are absorbing that burden, to the tune of about $25.60 per hour and 129.8 kilowatts of cooling load. That cooling cost is a direct subsidy from your mechanical systems to your electrical inefficiency. The total waste cost for June was approximately $33,460. The month averaged 476.6 volts — already below the 480-volt nominal. But within that average, we see seven annotated drop events, most landing in the 454 to 461 volt range. For motor-driven loads and variable frequency drives on a 480-volt bus, operating 19 to 25 volts below nominal on a recurring basis creates elevated winding stress and increases the probability of nuisance trips during production. The event that stands out most is the sag to 357.5 volts recorded near the start of June. That is a drop of over 120 volts below nominal — approximately 25 percent under the design voltage of the system. At that level, drives can trip offline, motor starters can fail to pick up, and in a food processing environment, process temperature control can be interrupted. This is not a tolerable operating condition, and its root cause needs to be investigated — whether it is a large motor starting without soft-start protection, a switching event on the distribution bus, or an upstream fault.
