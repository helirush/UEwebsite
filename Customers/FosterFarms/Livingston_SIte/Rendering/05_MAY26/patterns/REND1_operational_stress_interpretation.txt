**REND1 — Operational Stress Profile | May 2026**

This field shows the classic signature of a well-utilized industrial transformer that has never received reactive compensation. Let's read the stress indicators together.

Transformer loading is at 72.8% average capacity, with peaks reaching 1,773.7 kVA against a 2,500 kVA nameplate — that's 70.9% peak loading. In absolute terms, this is within the transformer's operating range, but the thermal burden of 880,313 BTU per hour tells you the transformer is running hot. A significant fraction of that thermal load is reactive-origin: the reactive current is circulating through every winding, every conductor, and every connection point in the system, generating I²R losses across the entire circuit. Reduce the reactive current and you directly reduce the thermal burden.

The cooling overhead is quantified in the BT header: approximately $25.32 per hour and 128.4 kW of cooling load — costing an estimated $221,823 per year just to manage the heat generated by a correctable inefficiency. That's not a small number. That's a dedicated cooling infrastructure cost that exists largely because the field is uncompensated.

The production rhythm in the BT and B views is clear — four to five production week blocks across May, with the field spending an estimated 55 to 60% of its operating time above the 1,399 kVA supply target line during active production shifts. This means the transformer is regularly operating above its daily planned supply capacity during peak production hours, which compounds the thermal and reactive stress during the hours when operational reliability matters most.

The two confirmed outage events in early May — brief but confirmed by both the gold streak visual indicators and the 9.5V minimum reading — are a reminder that this field is not operating with margin to spare. An uncompensated, thermally stressed transformer field has less resilience to voltage disturbances and switching events. Reactive correction directly improves that margin.
