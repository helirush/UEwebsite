Let me take you through the full engineering picture on the Deli F transformer — because every measurement dimension in this June capture is telling the same story, and it's worth understanding how they connect. Start with the reactive demand profile. The average kVAR across the thirty-day window is 407.1 — that's the baseline circulating reactive load running on this transformer every hour of every production day. At peak, that climbs to 750.7 kVAR. Now, reactive power is often described in abstract terms, but here's what it means concretely: reactive current is real current flowing through real conductors, heating real windings, and loading a real transformer — without producing any usable kilowatt output. It's the portion of apparent power that generates thermal stress but delivers nothing to your production floor. The power factor tells you the efficiency ratio. At 86.1 percent average, you're running below the threshold where reactive demand starts becoming expensive in multiple simultaneous ways. But the minimum power factor is the more important number for equipment health: 73.38 percent. At that floor, this transformer is operating at less than three-quarters efficiency during production surges. The gap between 73 percent and unity is where I²R heating lives — it scales with the square of the current, which means it compounds quickly as reactive demand rises. That's where the thermal burden connects. At 364,267 BTU per hour, Deli F is radiating enough heat to require 53.4 kilowatts of dedicated cooling infrastructure running continuously. At approximately $10.67 per hour, that cooling cost is a direct and ongoing tax on reactive inefficiency. In a food processing environment where refrigeration and ambient thermal management are already primary operating costs, that electromagnetic heat burden adds to cooling loads that were never designed to absorb it. Now layer in voltage instability. The 480-volt nominal field is swinging between 461 and 500 volts — a 39-volt operational range. The 461-volt minimum is 19 volts below nominal, recurring throughout production shifts across all four weeks of the capture. The triangular drop pattern — sharp undervoltage spike on load surge, immediate recovery — is the visual signature of reactive demand pulling the supply voltage down at the moment of peak inductive draw. Without local reactive compensation, that voltage sag propagates to every piece of equipment fed from this transformer. Variable frequency drives, soft-start controllers, compressor motors — they are all absorbing those 461-volt events, repeatedly, every production day. The harmonic picture adds another dimension. iTHD averages 5.3 percent and peaks at 10.3 percent. IEEE 519 sets the recommended industrial threshold at approximately 5 percent at the point of common coupling. Your average is right at that line and your peak is running double it. Harmonics at 10 percent THD contribute to transformer core heating through eddy current and hysteresis losses — this is separate from and additive to the resistive heating from reactive current. They also stress neutral conductors and accelerate insulation aging in motor windings. The MPTS simulation projects a direct corrective response to all of this.
