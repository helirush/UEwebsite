**DELIF — Projected MPTS Correction Outcome | Planning Estimate**

The S view for Deli F projects what this transformer's field looks like after MPTS correction is applied. The headline change is power factor: from 85.3 percent in the baseline to 99.5 percent in the corrected state. That single number captures almost everything that matters about the correction.

At 99.5 percent power factor, the reactive kVAR circulating through your system drops from a monthly average of 407.4 kVAR — with peaks at 726.7 kVAR — to near-zero residual reactive draw. The green unconsumed kVAR layer that sits visibly thick under every production block in the baseline view essentially disappears. The field is flowing cleanly, delivering real work instead of circulating waste.

The equipment sizing for this correction is 2 H490 MPTS units. At 400 kVAR each, two H490 units provide 800 kVAR of reactive compensation capacity — fully covering the 726.7 kVAR MAX this field demands at its worst. The planning-level investment estimate is approximately $200,000 installed, though final pricing will depend on engineering scope, site configuration, and installation conditions.

The waste that correction recovers is substantial. The baseline annual waste estimate is approximately $276,493 per year, with thermal burden contributing 361,073 BTU per hour and cooling overhead alone adding roughly $92,299 annually. MPTS correction addresses the root cause of all three of those cost streams — the reactive component — by neutralizing it at the source rather than managing its symptoms downstream.

Voltage stability improves in a corrected field. The reactive draw that currently pulls voltage down during load transitions is no longer circulating, which means the triangular dip events in the 458 to 461 volt range become less frequent and less severe. Device stress on motors, drives, and compressor contactors decreases, extending equipment service life and reducing maintenance intervals.

The practical outcome for Deli F is a transformer that is delivering more real work per kilovolt-amp of apparent power, running cooler, maintaining tighter voltage during load transitions, and costing less to operate every hour of every production shift.
