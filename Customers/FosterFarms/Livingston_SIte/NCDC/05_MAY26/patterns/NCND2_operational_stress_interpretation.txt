**NCDC2 — Operational Stress Interpretation | May 2026**

When you look at NCDC2's May field in its totality, you are seeing a transformer carrying the combined stress of three distinct and interacting burden sources: reactive circulation loss, thermal accumulation, and periodic complete field collapse. In isolation, any one of these would be manageable. Together, in an untuned field, they compound each other in ways that are expensive today and progressively more expensive over time.

The reactive burden — 244.9 kVAR average, peaking at 351.6 kVAR — is not static. It cycles with production load. Every time a large compressor stages on in the cold storage section, or a rendering line motor starts, the reactive demand spikes upward. The power factor drops momentarily toward or below that 93.0 percent average. The transformer core sees a momentary increase in magnetizing current draw. That current generates heat. That heat accumulates in the insulation system. At 167,322 BTU per hour of thermal burden, this transformer is running warm — and warm transformers age faster than cool ones. The insulation degradation curve is not linear; it accelerates as temperature rises, which means an already-stressed thermal environment in an untuned field tends to get worse, not better, over successive months.

The 4 to 5 outage events observed across May add a qualitatively different stress layer. Each complete field collapse followed by restoration puts every connected motor through a full inrush cycle simultaneously. On a facility of this scale — with a peak demand of 1,001.6 kilowatts and an average amperage of 807.3 amps — a simultaneous multi-motor inrush event is a significant mechanical and thermal shock to the system. Motor windings, contactor contacts, and variable frequency drive input stages all absorb that inrush energy. Over repeated events across months and years of operation, this kind of episodic stress is a documented contributor to premature motor and drive failure — the kind of failure that shows up as an unplanned maintenance event with real production downtime costs.

The cooling cost layer — $4.82 per hour, consuming 24.4 kilowatts of electrical capacity just to manage the heat this field generates — is the most straightforward operational expense in the picture. That is approximately $42,227 per year in cooling costs alone, just for this transformer's thermal burden. Every kilowatt of reactive current that MPTS correction eliminates from this field is a kilowatt that no longer needs to be cooled. The cooling cost line moves directly with the reactive burden, which means MPTS correction does not just recover energy waste — it reduces the active infrastructure cost of managing this transformer's heat output.

The total waste figure — approximately $127,148 per year — is the annual price of leaving this field untuned. Without a correction assignment, that number does not hold flat. Load mix at food processing facilities shifts seasonally. Summer cooling loads in California's Central Valley increase reactive demand on refrigeration compressors. Aging motor insulation increases resistive losses. The untuned field drifts toward higher waste, not lower, as time passes.
