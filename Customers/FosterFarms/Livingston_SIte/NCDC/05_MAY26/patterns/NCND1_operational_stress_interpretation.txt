**NCND1 — West11c Transformer | Operational Stress Interpretation**

West11c is operating as a high-utilization, continuous-operation transformer serving what appears to be a food processing and cold storage complex with around-the-clock operational demands. The load profile confirms this — there is no meaningful weekend drawdown visible in the 31-day dataset. The supply kVA band stays relatively flat across all days, the power factor stays consistently in the 80 to 90 percent range throughout, and the amperage holds steady. This is a facility that does not sleep, and neither does its electrical burden.

The operational stress on this transformer accumulates from three converging sources. First, the reactive burden of 267.9 kVAR average is adding constant thermal load to the windings — every hour of every day, the transformer is absorbing heat it would not need to absorb if the field were corrected. Second, the voltage disturbance events — particularly the near-zero collapse recorded in early May — represent high-impact transient stress events for the motor fleet and drive systems connected to this bus. Third, the cooling and decay overhead of 40.5 kilowatts and $7.96 per hour is a pure waste cost, infrastructure running continuously just to manage the thermal consequences of an uncorrected field.

From a production reliability standpoint, the two outage or severe disruption events identified in the data carry real operational risk. A food processing line that experiences an unexpected trip during peak production doesn't just lose the energy — it loses product, it triggers HACCP documentation requirements, it risks cold chain integrity for refrigerated inventory, and it generates maintenance labor hours to investigate and restore. Each of those consequences multiplies the apparent cost of the disruption well beyond what shows up in an energy bill.

The transformer itself — at 29 percent of nameplate capacity — has headroom before it becomes a thermal bottleneck. But the reactive burden is accelerating aging of the insulation system and the connected motor fleet at a rate faster than the nameplate utilization number suggests. The practical operational risk here is not imminent transformer failure — it is incremental, compounding degradation of connected equipment, punctuated by unexpected disruption events that will grow more frequent as insulation ages. This is a field that is manageable today but trending in the wrong direction without correction.
