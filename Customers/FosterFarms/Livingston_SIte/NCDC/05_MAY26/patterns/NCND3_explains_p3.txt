Let me take you through NCDC3 — the NorthE transformer — in full technical detail, because this field has several compounding issues that all connect to each other, and understanding the connections is what drives the right engineering response. Start with the reactive burden, because that's the root of most of what we're seeing. Over the full 31 days of May, NCDC3 NorthE carried an average reactive demand of 296.8 kVAR. The peak reached 379.4 kVAR. These aren't brief surge conditions — this reactive load is structural. It lives in the continuous inductive draw from refrigeration compressors, pump motors, conveyor drives, and every other motor-driven load on this bus. At 84.6 percent power factor average, roughly 15 percent of the total kVA flowing through this transformer is doing no productive work. It's reactive current — circulating, generating magnetic field losses, heating transformer windings, heating motor windings, and appearing on your utility meter as demand that you're paying for but not converting into output. The kVA supply target for NCDC3 is 664 kVA. During active production periods, the supplied kVA runs in the 500 to 650 kVA range — right up against that ceiling. Peak demand hit 738.2 kilowatts. When you're running a transformer that close to its supply ceiling with an 84.6 percent power factor, you're leaving very little margin for demand excursions — and in May, you had exactly those excursions, triggered by two power interruption events. Those outage events deserve their own explanation. The BT view identified two distinct gold-streak interruption signatures — one around May 14 to 15, a second near May 17. Combined estimated duration is approximately 3 to 6 minutes. In isolation, that sounds trivial. But in a food processing facility with cold-chain dependencies, a two-to-four-minute power interruption triggers a simultaneous restart sequence across your entire compressor bank and motor population on that bus. Each of those motors pulls five to seven times its normal operating current during restart inrush. All of them do it at the same time. The transformer sees a combined inrush kVA demand spike that can temporarily exceed its rating ceiling — and that spike deposits concentrated thermal stress into the transformer core and winding insulation that doesn't fully dissipate before the next production cycle begins.
