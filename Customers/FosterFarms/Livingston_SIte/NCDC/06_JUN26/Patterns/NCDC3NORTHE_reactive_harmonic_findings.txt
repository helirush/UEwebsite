That reactive floor never disappears across the entire thirty-day capture — it's present every day, every shift, including the reduced-staffing periods where production pulls back. Refrigeration loads are the primary driver here: compressors are inductive machines, and they demand reactive current continuously regardless of throughput. The transformer is essentially maintaining a standing reactive tax on its own capacity around the clock.

The harmonic profile adds another layer. The purple iTHD trace reads 5.8% average with an 11.4% peak maximum. Those harmonic spikes — the moments where the purple trace jumps upward — correlate directly and temporally with the same clustering zones where the triangular voltage drops appear in the TV view. This is not coincidental. Large inductive switching events — compressor starts, motor engagements — generate simultaneous harmonic injection and voltage depression. The field sees both effects at once: a reactive surge and a distortion burst, landing on the same timestamp. That temporal correlation is the signature of heavy-motor inductive switching, and it's consistent with what a facility of this type and scale should produce.

The combination — 83.7% power factor, 308.2 kVAR average reactive burden, 5.8% iTHD average, and 790A peak current — adds up to a transformer working significantly harder than its active power delivery requires. That excess effort is not invisible: it shows up as 333,852 BTU/hr of thermal burden and $84,164 per year in cooling costs just to manage the heat that the reactive burden generates.
