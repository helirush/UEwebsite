**BNR-1 — Operational Stress Interpretation | June 2026**

Pulling the voltage, reactive, harmonic, and load data together into an integrated operational stress picture, BNR-1 is a transformer under real and continuous industrial pressure — managed meaningfully by the existing MPTS correction, but not yet at the stable optimized operating state that the TBT target defines.

The combination of a 1,918.5-amp average current draw, a 692.6 kVAR average reactive burden, and a chronically sub-nominal voltage average of 476.5 volts creates a compound stress environment. High current flow elevates conductor I-squared-R losses and winding thermal loading. Reactive burden forces the transformer to carry circulating current that does no productive work but fully contributes to heat generation. Sub-nominal voltage causes connected motors to draw proportionally higher current to maintain torque output, which compounds the current stress further. These three mechanisms reinforce each other, and on a transformer regularly running near 1,961 amps under a 2,500 kVA nameplate, the headroom is tighter than the nameplate alone suggests.

The thermal burden reading of 602,911 BTU per hour is the physical expression of all of that compound stress. Running over 600,000 BTU per hour of heat generation in a food processing environment — where ambient temperatures are already managed aggressively for product quality and safety — means the cooling infrastructure serving this transformer room or electrical space is carrying a load that matters in both energy cost and equipment longevity terms. The 88.4 kilowatts of cooling load at approximately $17.55 per hour is an ongoing operational cost that directly tracks the field correction gap.

The outage events — estimated at two to three distinct events totaling roughly 30 to 90 minutes across the month — are individually modest in duration but operationally significant. In a continuous cold-chain operation, any unplanned voltage interruption triggers product temperature excursions, automated safety shutdowns, and restart sequencing that consumes additional energy and places inrush stress on motors and drives during the recovery cycle. Each restart event is also visible in the voltage trace as a triangular inrush drop.

The power factor minimum of 89.36 percent — meaning even at worst, the field is not collapsing into deeply uncorrected territory — is the clearest confirmation that the installed MPTS assets are doing genuine protective work for BNR-1 right now. The optimization opportunity is the gap between that 89.7 percent average and the 99-plus percent target state. Closing that gap reduces current, reduces heat, reduces cooling costs, and reduces the voltage depression that is driving the device-stress risk picture.
