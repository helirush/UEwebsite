At 86.2% average power factor, your transformer is pushing out more apparent power — kilovolt-amperes — than your production load is consuming in real kilowatts. The gap between those two numbers is reactive power, measured in kVAR. Your reactive average is 153.4 kVAR — that is the persistent background drag the field carries every production hour. But the number that matters most for sizing is the reactive maximum: 2,197.8 kVAR. That is the peak reactive demand your field generates during load transition events — compressor banks starting, large motors ramping, production systems coming online simultaneously. At nearly fifteen times the average reactive level, these spikes are short but impactful. They drive voltage sags down to 454 volts, they inject reactive current pulses into the transformer windings, and they produce the episodic power factor floor of 77.32% that the M-view records at its worst moments. The thermal consequence of running this untuned field is not abstract. At 147,430 BTU per hour of thermal burden, your transformer insulation is experiencing continuous elevated temperature. Transformer insulation life follows a well-established thermal aging curve — roughly every 10-degree Celsius increase in operating temperature cuts insulation life expectancy in half. The reactive current cycling through uncompensated windings is a constant, low-grade heat source that does exactly this. Your cooling systems are spending 21.5 kilowatts — about $4.25 per hour — to fight that heat. Over a year, that cooling overhead alone runs to a meaningful fraction of your total waste figure. The voltage field adds another stress dimension. Your operating range this month ran from 454.1 volts at the floor to 507.8 volts at the ceiling — an 11.2% swing around a 480-volt nominal. The elevated ceiling is a utility feed characteristic: lightly regulated supply voltage rises when load is light and sags when load is heavy. The floor excursions — particularly the clustering of events in the 260520 through 260526 window — are where the operational risk lives. A 5.4% under-nominal dip on a facility running refrigeration compressors, conveyors, and processing equipment reduces motor starting torque, increases slip, and drives winding temperatures upward at exactly the moment when the motors are already under mechanical stress. Reactive compensation partially buffers these sag events by reducing the reactive current component during load starts — meaning the voltage holds better when correction is in place. The harmonic content — 5.0% average iTHD, 10.3% maximum — is persistent but not dominant. Variable frequency drives and motor controllers are the likely contributors. This level of harmonic content adds to the total thermal burden and contributes to waveform distortion stress on sensitive controls, but it does not change the primary correction prescription: reactive compensation is the first and largest lever available on this field.
