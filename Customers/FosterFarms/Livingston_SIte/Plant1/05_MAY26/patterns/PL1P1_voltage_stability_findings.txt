**PL1P1 — Voltage Health | May 2026**

The voltage story on PL1P1 carries a serious flag that cannot be minimized. The nominal operating voltage for this transformer is 481.4 volts, and the maximum recorded voltage for the month was 503.7 volts — modestly elevated but within acceptable range. The minimum recorded voltage, however, was 9.7 volts. That is not a data annotation error. A recorded floor of 9.7 volts on a 480-volt industrial system represents a near-complete field collapse event. It means this transformer's field dropped to essentially zero at least once during May.

What that kind of event does to connected equipment is significant. Motor windings that collapse to near-zero voltage and then recover without a controlled restart sequence are subjected to an inrush current event on recovery that can be six to ten times normal operating current. For variable frequency drives, control electronics, and soft-starter systems, that kind of uncontrolled collapse-and-recovery cycle is exactly the mechanism that triggers insulation breakdown and premature component failure. One event of this severity would be noteworthy. In the context of the other voltage behavior in this dataset, it is a warning.

The bulk of the voltage trace across May holds reasonably steady in the upper operating band, clustering near and just below the 480-volt reference line. But the field is not flat. The blue voltage trace throughout the month shows continuous micro-variation — a persistently rippling band rather than a stable line. This pattern is characteristic of a heavy industrial environment where large motor loads and refrigeration compressors cycle on and off repeatedly, each event sending a small perturbation through the voltage field. Over time, that continuous micro-stress is cumulative on insulation systems.

Triangular voltage-drop events — the characteristic collapse-and-recovery shape that indicates inductive load switching — are present and recurring throughout the month. They cluster most densely in the middle two weeks of May, around the May 11th through 14th window, suggesting that the facility's heavier cycling loads were operating at elevated frequency during that period. These drops are not isolated anomalies. They are a systemic behavioral pattern in this field, and they are occurring in a transformer that is already carrying a reactive burden and a thermal load that have not been compensated.
