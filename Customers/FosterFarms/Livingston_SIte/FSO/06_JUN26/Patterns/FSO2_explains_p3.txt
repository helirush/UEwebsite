The maximum recorded is 95.5 percent — showing the field can touch near-optimal conditions — but the monthly average of 91.6 percent tells you the compensation profile is not consistently delivering at the top of its range. The gap between 91.6 and the 99.5 percent optimization target translates directly to 96.4 kVAR of average reactive demand, with a maximum event reaching 152.1 kVAR. That reactive current is flowing through your transformer windings, your cables, and your switchgear continuously. It is doing no real work. Combined with the reactive losses themselves, the total estimated annual waste for FSO2 in its current tuned-but-not-fully-optimized state is approximately $58,500 per year. The voltage story in June adds a second dimension to the stress picture. The TV view shows the voltage trace holding a stable upper band for most of the month — maximum of 498.4 volts, confirming occasional mild swells but nothing alarming on the high side. The concern is the floor. The operational voltage minimum visible in the chart body is approximately 460 volts — 20 volts below the 480-volt nominal reference for this system. In a food processing environment, that floor is not background noise. Motors drawing current at 460 volts instead of 480 are pulling approximately 4 percent more current to maintain torque — that additional current means more heat in the windings, more thermal cycling in the bearings, and a statistically shorter mean time between failures. Across a month of operations, and across multiple machines simultaneously, that cumulative undervoltage exposure shortens your equipment service life in a measurable way. The voltage drops are structured and concentrated. The triangular drop pattern clusters most densely in the 260607 through 260616 window — the second and third weeks of June. These are not random noise. They are the signature of large inductive loads switching on and pulling the bus down before recovery occurs. The most likely candidates in this facility are large refrigeration compressor starts, high-inertia motor loads, or groups of VFD-driven equipment switching simultaneously. Three distinct outage-level interruption events were also identified — narrow but definitive production stops visible in both the TBT and TV views, most clearly around June 7, June 19, and a lesser event near June 25. Total estimated downtime across the month is approximately 4 to 8 minutes. Brief — but each event is a full field-collapse and recovery cycle for every piece of power electronics downstream, including every VFD, every PLC, and every soft starter in the building. The harmonic picture from the TM view ties the voltage and reactive findings together. Current total harmonic distortion averages 5.6 percent, with maximum events reaching 10.6 percent. Critically, those maximum harmonic events are time-correlated with the voltage drop events — they appear at the same points on the timeline. That co-occurrence confirms a single root cause: specific load-switching events that simultaneously pull voltage down and inject harmonic content. Knowing that both problems share a source is actually useful information — it means a single targeted diagnostic sweep of those loads could address both the harmonic injection and the voltage depression in one engineering action. The thermal burden drops substantially from the 72,923 BTU per hour current level. The cooling overhead is significantly reduced. The voltage bus stiffens — reactive compensation at full optimization reduces the magnitude of the triangular voltage drops, raising the effective floor from 460 volts toward nominal and reducing the cumulative device stress on every motor and power electronics component downstream.
