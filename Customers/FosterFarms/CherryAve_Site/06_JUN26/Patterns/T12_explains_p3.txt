Let me walk you through T12 in full, because this transformer deserves a complete picture — and the June data gives us one. Start with the fundamentals. T12 is serving active food processing and cold storage operations, and it's doing so at scale. Peak real demand is 1,631.1 kilowatts. The daily supply target is 1,709 kVA — and your operating pattern shows approximately 65 to 70 percent of weekday hours running at or above that target. This is a working transformer, heavily utilized, serving a production calendar that doesn't leave much idle time on the weekdays. Now here's the problem. That 1,631-kilowatt real load is accompanied by a reactive demand that averages 712.9 kVAR and peaks at 1,043.5 kVAR. Reactive power — kVAR — is the component of electrical current that does no real work. It magnetizes motor windings, energizes transformer cores, and sustains HVAC and refrigeration field coils. It is necessary for those loads to function, but it is fundamentally wasteful when it has to travel from the utility through the transformer rather than being sourced locally. And on T12, all of it is traveling through the transformer — because there is no local reactive compensation in service. The field is untuned. The consequence of that uncompensated reactive current shows up in three compounding ways. First, thermal burden. Every ampere of reactive current generates resistive I-squared-R heating in transformer windings and distribution conductors. T12's thermal burden measurement is 848,819 BTU per hour — with a baseline floor of 725,439 BTU per hour even at off-peak moments. That is continuous, around-the-clock heat baking into insulation systems, motor windings, and panel infrastructure. Insulation degradation is slow but it is directional and it does not reverse. Every month without correction adds incrementally to the accumulated thermal stress on every piece of equipment this transformer serves. Second, cooling overhead. That 848,819 BTU per hour has to go somewhere. Your facility's cooling systems are working against it constantly, at a cost of approximately $35.56 per operating hour — approximately $311,485 per year — just to manage heat that reactive compensation would largely eliminate. Third, voltage instability. The reactive current demand — particularly the sharp inrush events when large compressor motors and conveyor drives come online — pulls the voltage bus down at transition moments. Your June voltage record shows nine confirmed dips below 480-volt nominal. The worst was 435.8 volts — 9.2 percent below nominal. That is the level where VFD drives begin exhibiting fault responses, where motor slip increases and current draw rises, and where sensitive control electronics begin accumulating stress cycle counts toward their failure thresholds.
